Reading workflow

How do I summarize a long article with AI without missing key details?

Define what the summary must help you understand, retain the source's qualifications, then verify important points in the article itself.

To summarize a long article without losing important details, decide what you need the summary for, provide the article or an authorized excerpt, and ask the AI to preserve the main claim together with its evidence, limitations, and unresolved questions. Then compare the summary with the original. A short output is not automatically a complete or faithful one.

Set a purpose before asking for a summary

"Summarize this" leaves the audience and purpose open. Instead, specify whether you need a neutral overview, a decision brief, a list of steps, or a record of findings. For a policy article, you may need the rule and exceptions; for a product announcement, the change, affected versions, and effective date; for a research article, the question, design, results, and limitations.

Set the output length only after deciding what it must preserve. A word target can encourage compression, but it cannot tell the model which distinctions matter. If the article contains several topics, request a structured summary with one section per topic rather than one paragraph that blends them.

Prepare the source with its identity intact

Include the title, publisher or author, date or version, and URL. Keep headings and captions that clarify the scope of a passage. When copying a section, include definitions, footnotes, table headers, and caveats that affect the meaning of nearby claims. If your AI tool can access a file or a webpage, confirm that the specific source was actually available to it.

Do not treat a link as proof that the article was read. If the page cannot be opened, supply material only when you have permission to do so. The guide to why a chat may not read a link covers access limits.

Request a traceable summary

Ask the AI to distinguish what the author states from interpretation and to mark missing information. One prompt pattern is:

Summarize the article for [purpose and audience].
Separate the author's main claim, supporting evidence, and limitations.
Keep dates, units, definitions, and exceptions with the claims they qualify.
For each important point, give a heading or short quote that helps me find it.
Mark information the article does not state. Do not fill gaps from memory.
Source: [title, author, date, URL, and supplied text]

The prompt helps make the requested structure explicit; it does not ensure that every claim will be captured or that references will be correct. For consequential use, follow each summary point back to the article.

Check for the details summaries often flatten

  • Scope: Does the summary describe the same population, time period, product version, or location as the article?
  • Evidence: Does the cited paragraph actually support the statement, or does it merely discuss the same topic?
  • Qualifications: Were caveats, exceptions, uncertainty, or counterarguments dropped?
  • Numbers: Are units, denominators, ranges, and dates intact?
  • Completeness: Did the answer skip a section that would change the conclusion?

Correct the summary against the source rather than asking the model to confirm its own answer. You can request a second pass that lists unsupported or weakly supported statements, but still inspect them yourself.

If the article is too long to process at once

Split it at meaningful section boundaries, give each part a source and section label, and ask for a short evidence note from each part. Synthesize those notes only after reviewing what each section says. Do not summarize isolated fragments and then lose the connection between a claim and its exception. For research publications, preserve methods and limitations alongside findings; the research-article guide covers that narrower task. For a broader prompt setup, see how to give an AI useful context.
