Source checking

How do I compare conflicting information from different websites?

Record each claim and its scope first, then investigate whether the disagreement is real, outdated, or about different situations.

When websites conflict, do not average their claims or choose the one that sounds most confident. Write down what each source says, when it was published or updated, whom or what it describes, and what evidence it gives. Then check definitions, dates, and scope before deciding whether the claims truly contradict each other. If they still disagree, preserve that uncertainty in your conclusion.

Compare like with like

Two numbers may refer to different versions, regions, populations, time periods, or measurement methods. A product's current support page may not match an old review because the product changed. A general rule may have exceptions for a particular account type. Record the exact terms each page uses before translating them into your own language.

Assess each source for the question at hand

Check who published the page, whether it is primary documentation or commentary, how current it is, and whether it shows evidence for the specific claim. A government notice may be a better source for a rule than a blog repeating it; an original study may be needed to assess a research method. Source quality is relevant to the claim, not a fixed ranking that applies to every topic.

Look for the original document or dataset behind a secondary report. If the page cites another source, follow the link and compare what the underlying material actually says. Several websites may repeat the same press release, so multiple pages are not automatically independent confirmation.

Build a disagreement table

For each source, capture the claim and evidence in separate columns. A short table can reveal that two sources use different conditions rather than disagreeing about the same fact.

Question | Source A | Source B | Difference to check
Effective date | Date in notice | Date in FAQ | Did the rule change?
Eligible group | New accounts | All accounts | Different scope?
Measured value | Annual total | Monthly average | Different unit or period?

This is a fictional example for illustrating comparison fields, not real policy or product information. Use the source's own terms and preserve the original units, population, and date.

Use an AI assistant without letting it erase the conflict

Supply labeled excerpts or confirm that the tool actually reached each page. Ask it to extract each source's claim separately, quote a short supporting passage, and identify differences in date, scope, definitions, or method. Tell it to report "unresolved" when the material does not establish which claim is correct. Then verify the quotations and citations yourself.

AI can help organize a comparison, but it may misread a source, miss a caveat, or produce an unsupported reconciliation. See how to ask questions about several webpages and how to request traceable citations.

State what you can and cannot conclude

A careful result may say that one source is newer, that two sources discuss different cases, or that there is not enough evidence to resolve a conflict. Explain the basis and provide links. For a high-impact decision, check with a qualified professional or the relevant authority rather than treating a web comparison as a final determination.

To keep the research trail, create a separate note for each source and retain its link, date, and section. The guide to organized research notes provides a reusable record structure.
